Faceted View of Tech Careers

The taxonomy organizes occupational knowledge from O*NET and grounded qualitative coding into four complementary facets. Each facet offers a different way to read a career profile, encouraging comparisons that go beyond job titles or employer-specific language. The structure supports transparent information architecture for the platform, so users can filter and combine facets to build an accurate understanding of work.

Knowledge Facet

The knowledge facet captures the disciplinary foundations that shape how work is reasoned about and justified. It covers domain knowledge such as computer science principles, project management frameworks, data ethics, or business process understanding. Knowledge statements reflect the conceptual vocabulary an occupation uses to diagnose problems, design solutions, and communicate rationale.

Skills Facet

The skills facet describes applied capabilities that enable practitioners to operate within teams and tools. It includes technical proficiencies like programming, data querying, requirements elicitation, and systems integration, as well as cross-functional abilities such as documentation, coordination, and iterative improvement. Skills are framed as observable actions rather than abstract traits.

Personality Facet

The personality facet highlights dispositions that affect how individuals approach uncertainty, collaboration, and learning. Traits such as analytical curiosity, dependability, adaptability, and attentiveness to detail surface repeatedly in the qualitative coding. The facet helps users see how preferred work styles align with specific occupational contexts.

Task / Role Orientation

Task and role orientation summarizes the kinds of responsibilities and deliverables associated with a role. It groups activities like building software artifacts, coordinating stakeholders, analyzing datasets, or stewarding project lifecycles. This facet situates careers within workflows and organizational expectations, linking day-to-day tasks to broader outcomes.

Comparing Across Facets

Because each career is annotated across the same facets, users can compare occupations on multiple dimensions at once. For example, two roles may share a programming skill set but differ in knowledge emphasis or personality fit. The faceted design makes these similarities and contrasts visible, supporting informed career exploration and curriculum design.
